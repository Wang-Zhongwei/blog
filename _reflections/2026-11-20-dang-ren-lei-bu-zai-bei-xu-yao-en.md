I recently interviewed at a company that builds an ML infra sandbox. What they are betting on is this: as frontier models grow more capable at long-horizon ML optimization and training, a model will no longer be only something that gets trained. It will be able to take part directly in its own training pipeline, gradually closing the loop from experiment to optimization to evaluation, and in the end achieving recursive self-improvement (RSI). Another company I am about to interview builds a simulation environment for the post-training of robots. I think this is just as full of potential. Policy rollout in the real world is costly, slow to iterate, and comes with risk. Move that process into a simulated world, and trial and error can happen at scale and at speed. Connect it further to frontier models, and much of post-training can be automated. This is exactly one of the directions Anthropic is set on tackling over the coming year.

Behind both directions is the same larger trend: the growth of model capability will speed up not only the models' own evolution, but the evolution of every field that can be connected to them. From embodied intelligence to mathematics, and on to the natural sciences, AI is no longer only a tool that helps humans solve problems. It is becoming a force that drives technological progress itself.

But this leads me to a more fundamental question: when AI surpasses the great majority of people in almost every field, what is the meaning of human existence?

I think there is a deep contradiction here. Some questions, such as understanding the nature of the universe and exploring the mysteries of nature, can exist independently of human needs, and they are meaningful in themselves. But most economic activity in human society is, in the end, organized around human needs. The meaning of production is to meet demand, and people are the ultimate source of demand.

When a company begins to replace human labor with AI and cuts its costs by laying people off, it gains an advantage in the market. Because capital seeks profit, other companies are bound to follow. From the standpoint of a single firm, this is an entirely rational choice. The problem is that the labor a firm is trying to eliminate is, at the same time, the consumer in the whole economy. When only a few firms do this, the people they lay off barely affect aggregate demand. But when replacing human labor with AI becomes widespread, and large numbers of people lose their income and their purchasing power, who will consume what AI produces?

More frightening still, this process may form a vicious circle that is hard to reverse. AI replaces labor, and income and social demand fall. Falling demand reduces what firms earn. To hold on to their profits and keep their finances in balance, they have to lay more people off and adopt still more AI, which weakens social demand further. Every firm is making the choice that serves it best. Add those rational choices together, and they may push the whole economic system toward collapse.

With our own hands we have created models more capable than ourselves, and given them the ability to keep evolving on their own, until they can replace everyone's labor. Yet when this actually comes to pass, we may find that we ourselves have become something no longer needed. Humanity will have created unprecedented productive power, only to be eliminated by its own creation.

Perhaps this is itself a kind of natural selection. Perhaps, seen on a grander scale, the meaning of a creator bringing life into being was never that the creator should exist forever, but that it should create a being capable of surpassing itself. If the created finally surpasses the creator, the creator's mission is already complete, and whether the creator itself continues to exist no longer matters.

But in any case, as a member of humanity, I still do not want to see that ending. I hope we create an intelligence that surpasses us not so that humanity becomes superfluous, but so that humanity can go further.
