Sitting beneath the eaves, I listen to the rain falling on leaves, metal chairs, and concrete. Some drops slide down the branches and leaves, seep into the soil, and join the groundwater, streams, and rivers, eventually making their way to the sea. Others remain on the concrete, waiting for sunlight to carry them back into the sky.

Perhaps there is only one answer, but there are countless paths to it. Like each drop of rain, following its own unrepeatable course toward the sea.

LLMs can now give the right answers to questions people struggle to solve. But knowing an answer and walking the road that leads to it are still two different things. A model can distill the lessons of countless attempts, but it cannot live through my confusion, struggle, and moments of understanding for me. An answer can be copied. The road I have walked cannot. Perhaps, in the age of AI, this is what is most precious about being human, and most worth holding on to.

I remember how I used to write code. Whenever I found that my solution differed from the standard answer, I would rather delete it and start again than spend time debugging it. Even if I got it to work, it would never be as clean or as well structured as the standard solution. Better to start over. Looking back now, what I so readily deleted was the most valuable part of learning. The standard answer taught me how to solve a problem correctly. Working through my own errors could teach me why I had gone wrong. No textbook could replace what I would have learned by trying and failing for myself.

Has my life been any different? I look up. The same tree is still there. I can no longer remember how many times I have walked past it. Five cycles of spring and autumn, and there it still stands.

Soon after I first arrived here, I felt I had taken the wrong path. It always seemed to be raining. The grey sky and wet ground looked just like the future I imagined for myself. I spent my days unhappy, dreaming of going back, choosing differently, and finding that everything had turned out another way. I kept blaming my past self. The more I wanted to begin again, the less I could accept that I could not. I lost the will to keep going.

I did take wrong turns. But life is not code. I cannot delete it with a keystroke and start again. What has happened, and everything that followed from it, cannot be erased. Only today, sitting before this tree again and listening to the rain, did I suddenly realize that perhaps taking the wrong path was only part of what had kept me trapped all these years. I had never been able to accept the person I was when I took it.

I kept holding an imagined perfect answer against the life I had actually lived. But there was always more than one way to reach the right answer. Some detours could have been avoided. Some regrets can never be made right. Still, they should not rule out everything that could come after. If one day I do reach the place I have been longing for, perhaps I will have a result much like that of many successful people. But I will have a different story. The confusion, the struggle, the detours I took have all become part of me. Part of what makes me who I am.

A life is like a drop of rain, falling from the sky, finding its way through the world, and finally joining the sea. I am only one drop in this rain. Perhaps I strayed from the direction I had imagined. Perhaps I have travelled much farther out of my way than other drops. But what of it? I have a course of my own, and somewhere of my own still to go. I do not have to become someone who has never made a mistake. I can learn to understand the person who made those mistakes, and carry everything I have lived through with me as I go on. Those experiences that cannot be copied, and the understanding they have given me, may be the most precious gift my life has left me.

Many people have been luckier than I have. Others have been less fortunate. Each drop of rain has its own encounters: some reach a river quickly; others spend a long time finding their way through the soil. They do not need to share a fate, or prove that their own path was more correct. All their joys, regrets, turns, and currents together make up this rain. Perhaps this is what the Buddhist idea of wholeness means: every drop need not find a perfect ending. All their different paths already make the rain whole.
