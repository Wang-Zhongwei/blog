The world has more and more information telling you how to do something. What remains scarce is someone asking you why you want to do it. Because no one can think that through for you.

Take OpenClaw, which was everywhere a little while ago. You see so many people building one that you feel the urge yourself: should I build one too? But before asking how to set it up, I need to ask why. What problem would it solve for me? And what does that problem have to do with what I want from my life in the longer term?

People may all be building OpenClaw, yet want entirely different things from it. Someone whose daily work is highly repetitive wants it to collect, organize, and summarize information, freeing up time spent on mechanical tasks. Someone else wants a daily report on a particular field, to steadily deepen their understanding. Some want the attention that comes with a trend. Others have no intention of building an application themselves; they would rather “sell shovels” and make money from the rush. On the surface, everyone is doing the same thing. In fact, they are heading to entirely different places.

The real danger is following others into something before you have a purpose of your own. Once you have no direction, every trend, every new tool, every choice that seems to have made someone else successful becomes another force pulling you somewhere else. Your time, attention, and energy dissipate across countless components. You seem to be moving all the time, yet there is no net displacement.

What looks like one activity is a superposition of many different purposes. Beyond the differences in execution, the deepest difference lies in the intention behind it.

Once you understand why you want to do something, you have a will to guide you. The energy that was scattered begins to gather around it, becoming a sustained force that carries you through the milestones of your life, until you finish writing the story that is yours.
